Problem Statement

An electrocardiogram (ECG) is the clinical standard for assessing the heart. Beyond heart rate, it resolves the shape of every beat, so a clinician can measure the RR, QRS, and QT intervals that cardiac assessment relies on. Recording one takes electrodes and a clinical setup, which makes it impractical for long-term monitoring. Photoplethysmography (PPG), the optical pulse sensor in most smartwatches, has the opposite profile. It is cheap, non-invasive, and worn all day, but it tracks changes in blood volume under the skin rather than the heart’s electrical activity, and on its own it has limited clinical value. Reconstructing the ECG from the PPG would bring clinical-grade cardiac information to a wearable.

Mathematically, this is an inverse problem. We observe a PPG y, produced from an unknown ECG x by a forward operator plus noise: y = H(x, z) + ε, where z collects patient-specific quantities such as the delay between a heartbeat and the pulse reaching the sensor. The operator is lossy. The vasculature acts as a low-pass filter that smooths away the sharp features of the QRS complex, and finite sampling, the sensor’s response, and noise remove more, so a single PPG is consistent with many different ECGs and there is no unique answer to recover. The Bayesian formulation asks for all of them instead: the posterior p(x | y) ∝ p(y | x) p(x), which weights each candidate ECG by how well it explains the measurement and how plausible it is as an ECG. The likelihood p(y | x) follows from the forward model. The prior p(x) has to capture what real ECGs look like and has no closed form, so we learn it with a diffusion model.

In clinical practice the uncertainty matters as much as the reconstruction. A QT interval pinned down to within a few milliseconds and one uncertain by tens of milliseconds are different findings, even when their best estimates agree, and only the first should inform a decision. That places two demands on the posterior. Its credible intervals have to be calibrated, so that a 90% interval contains the true value 90% of the time. And it should report where its uncertainty comes from: the PPG leaving the ECG undetermined, the patient’s physiology being poorly identified, or the models themselves having been fit to finite data. The first is a property of the measurement that no amount of training data removes; the last shrinks as data grows.

Approach

Earlier PPG-to-ECG models, built on GANs, diffusion, and flows, return a single waveform with no uncertainty. The closest uncertainty-aware method has no explicit forward operator or separable prior, and does not report calibration. Our method, LUCID (Likelihood-Uncertainty-Calibrated Inverse Diffusion), is built from three pieces that no existing approach combines: a diffusion prior over ECGs that is trained once and reused, an explicit physiological forward operator, and Bayesian posterior inference whose uncertainty is calibrated and attributed to its sources.

A forward model with four unknowns

Instead of learning a black-box map from PPG to ECG, we write the forward operator down. It is semi-physiological, a composition of steps that each have a physical reading: H(x, z) = a · S[h ∗ r(x)](t − τ) + b. A pointwise nonlinearity r reshapes the ECG sample by sample. Convolution with a smoothing kernel h plays the part of the vasculature’s low-pass filter, and it is the step where information is lost. S corrects residual sensor distortion, τ is the pulse-transit delay, and a and b set gain and offset. With Gaussian noise, the likelihood is a Gaussian centered on H(x, z).

Reconstructing one recording then involves four unknowns, arranged in a hierarchy. The ECG x is the target. The physiology z (the kernel’s shape, the delay, the gain, and the offset) is specific to each recording. The operator parameters φ (the nonlinearity, the sensor correction, and the noise covariance) are shared across patients and estimated from paired ECG–PPG recordings. And θ, the weights of the diffusion prior, were themselves learned from a finite set of ECGs. Each is uncertain for a different reason, and the method keeps them separate throughout.

Sampling the posterior

The posterior over all four unknowns has no closed form, so we sample from it, in two stages. Offline, we train K = 10 diffusion priors, each on a bootstrap resample of the ECG-only patients. A single trained model is one noisy estimate of the true distribution of ECGs; the spread across the ensemble is what lets the posterior carry uncertainty about the prior itself. The forward operator gets a distribution too, a variational approximation q(φ) fit to the paired recordings by maximizing the evidence lower bound. Both are frozen before any PPG is seen.

At inference, each prior runs its own Markov chain, and every iteration updates the remaining unknowns in turn, each with a kernel suited to it. The operator parameters φ are proposed from q(φ) and accepted by a Metropolis–Hastings test, which reduces to a likelihood ratio because q(φ) also stands in for their prior. The physiology z is low-dimensional and differentiable, so it moves by the Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm, a step along the gradient of its log-posterior followed by an accept–reject correction. The ECG x is updated by annealed diffusion MCMC: at each diffusion step, the reverse process is steered toward the measurement by adding the likelihood gradient to the prior’s score. That gradient is evaluated at the model’s denoised estimate of the clean ECG and rescaled against the prior score, so the two stay in balance. Each chain runs for 100 iterations and discards the first 20 as burn-in.

The chains are then pooled, but not equally. Each prior’s evidence, the probability it assigns to the observed PPG, is estimated by tempered sequential Monte Carlo, because the likelihood is too sharply peaked for plain Monte Carlo. Bayes’ rule turns the evidence into weights, and the final posterior is the weighted mixture of the ten chains.

Attributing the uncertainty

For any clinical quantity computed from the ECG, such as an RR, QRS, or QT interval, the posterior variance splits exactly into four nonnegative terms, one per level of the hierarchy. The split is the law of total variance applied in nested layers, conditioning on the prior, then the operator, then the physiology. It yields a prior term, an operator term, a physiology term, and an ECG term for what remains: the ambiguity the PPG leaves in the ECG even when everything else is known. All four are computed from the weighted samples the algorithm already returns.

We also bound, in total variation, how far the sampler’s output can be from the exact posterior, and carry the bound through to coverage: any credible interval built from the samples misses its nominal level by at most the same amount. The bound breaks into named errors: how well the bootstrap ensemble represents the prior, a KL term for the variational operator, and computational terms for chain mixing, the evidence weights, score and guidance error, and discretization. When calibration fails, the decomposition points to the approximation to improve.

Preliminary Results

The experiments so far are controlled. The priors are trained on PTB-XL, and test PPGs are generated by passing held-out ECGs through the forward operator at four noise levels, from 5 to 40 dB SNR, so the true ECG and physiology are known exactly. Each level has 20 test windows.

Less noise makes the reconstructions fit the PPG more closely: the misfit between the observed PPG and the one predicted from the reconstruction falls from 0.30 at 5 dB to 0.05 at 40 dB. It barely narrows the posterior, though. The average posterior standard deviation stays between 0.51 and 0.58 at every noise level, and the decomposition assigns nearly all of the variance to the ECG term, which is the pattern to expect if the ambiguity comes from what the smoothing destroys rather than from sensor noise. The physiology term is at least a hundred times smaller, consistent with the delay, gain, and offset being recovered well.

The raw posterior is over-confident: its nominal 90% intervals contained the truth in only 75–83% of windows. Inflating the posterior spread by about 1.3×, tuned on a separate calibration split, brings 90% coverage to 87.5–93.3% at every noise level and 95% coverage to 90–95%. The same factor over-covers at the 50% level (67–78%), which says the posterior’s shape is off and not only its width. Its uncertainty is informative, too: at 10 dB, windows with a wider posterior tended to be reconstructed worse (r = 0.39 across 20 windows), though at p = 0.09 that is suggestive rather than established.

Limitations

Everything above is semi-synthetic. The test PPGs come from the same forward operator the sampler assumes, so the likelihood is correct by construction, and the operator parameters were held fixed, so the operator term is zero by design rather than by measurement. Evaluation on real paired recordings from VitalDB, and comparison against existing PPG-to-ECG models (RDDM, CardioGAN, and PPG-flow), are still to come. Twenty windows per noise level are too few to settle whether uncertainty predicts error. Calibration currently leans on a post-hoc inflation factor, and the coverage bound is stated in terms of approximation errors we have not yet estimated, so for now it explains where calibration can break rather than certifying that it holds.
