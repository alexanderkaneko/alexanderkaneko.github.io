About

This was my independent research project at the Massachusetts Academy of Math and Science, five months of work from September 2022 to February 2023, advised by Dr. Kevin Crowthers and Mr. Nick Medeiros. The starting point was that an electric vehicle is only as clean as the grid behind it. It moves emissions from the tailpipe to the power plant rather than removing them, so anything that cuts the electricity a fleet draws also cuts emissions. Most of the energy a car wastes on a trip goes into stopping and starting, and traffic signals decide how much of that there is.

Signal optimization is a well-worked problem, but almost all of it minimizes travel time, and what drives an EV’s energy use is not what drives delay. A plan that keeps cars rolling slowly can beat one that sprints them between hard stops. Li et al. (2015) had shown how to plan routes and signal timings at the same time by folding both onto a single space–phase–time hypernetwork, but their objective was travel time too, and they left it as a framework rather than a working system.

So I built the energy version: a centralized controller that assigns every vehicle a route and every intersection a phase sequence in one solve, with electricity as the objective. I then measured it against the status quo and against a travel-time controller, inside a real microsimulator. Over 50 randomized demand cases it cut average consumption by 11% against fixed-time signals and 4.5% against the travel-time formulation, taking about three seconds to plan each case.

Approach

Both problems on one graph

The construction, taken from Li et al., expands the network along time. In the space–time layer a node is a (place, second) pair, so a vehicle’s route is a path through that layer. An arc from (O, t) to (N₁, t+3) means traverse the link and arrive three seconds later; a horizontal arc means wait in place. In the phase–time layer a node is a (phase, second) pair, and an arc from (P₁, t) to (P₂, t+Δ) means hold P₁ green for Δ seconds, then switch. Δ was bounded to 7–20 s to keep green splits realistic.

One condition couples the layers: a vehicle may cross a signalized node only in a second when a compatible phase holds the green. That coupling is what makes the joint problem hard. Routing is straightforward once the signal plan is fixed, and the signal plan is straightforward once the routes are. Everything was discretized to one-second steps over a 100-second planning horizon.

Relaxing the coupling

I solved the problem by Lagrangian relaxation. The coupling and capacity constraints move out of the constraint set and into the objective, each multiplied by a price λ charged when it is violated. Once the constraints are priced rather than enforced, the problem separates into two independent least-cost path problems, one per layer. Both are solved exactly by forward dynamic programming: the algorithm sweeps forward through time and settles the label on each (node, second) once, so a route costs one pass over the horizon rather than an enumeration of paths.

The prices start uniform. Each iteration corrects them from what the previous plan actually did.

Simulation in the loop

Each candidate solution is executed in a microsimulator rather than scored by an estimation function. I compared SUMO against DTALite and NetLogo on a decision matrix and chose SUMO for its electric-vehicle models and per-edge electricity accounting. It runs from Python over the TraCI API: the controller writes the phase it wants onto each signal every second, then reads back the vehicle count and the watt-hours drawn on every link.

Those readings set the next iteration’s prices. A link’s multiplier rises with the flow it carried, falls with the capacity it had left, and rises with that link’s share of the network’s total electricity. The third term is what makes it an energy model. Links where cars draw power become expensive, so the next solve routes around them and re-times the signals to keep traffic off them. Each case ran thirty of these rounds at a fixed step size.

The travel-time controller used as a baseline is the same program with that third term deleted. Network, demand, solver, and simulator are identical, so the comparison isolates the objective rather than the implementation.

Results

I ran three controllers over the same 50 randomized demand cases, varying congestion level, origins, and departure times: fixed-time signals as the status quo, the travel-time formulation, and the energy-aware model. The response variable is mean electricity per vehicle in watt-hours, as reported by SUMO. Every controller sees the same cases, so the comparisons are paired.

Against fixed-time signals the energy-aware controller averaged 179.4 Wh per vehicle versus 201.5, a mean saving of 22.2 Wh or 11.0% (one-sided paired t-test, t = 6.70, p = 9.7 × 10⁻⁹). Against the travel-time formulation the margin is smaller: 187.9 Wh down to 179.4, a saving of 4.5% (t = 2.27, p = 0.014). The second comparison is the informative one. Two controllers with identical machinery and different objectives did not produce the same plan, which means minimizing delay is not a substitute for minimizing energy.

Against fixed-time signals the model was ahead in 43 of the 50 cases and level in two; the five losses were all under 15 Wh, while the gains reach 96 Wh. Against the travel-time controller the split is 28 wins to 15 losses with seven cases level, and the largest gain of 117 Wh is more than three times the largest loss of 34 Wh.

Runtime

Solving one case took 3.24 s on average with a standard deviation of 0.41 s, and 4.22 s on the slowest of the fifty, measured on a MacBook Air M2 running single-threaded Python. Earlier versions took minutes. Most of the gain came from replacing repeated path enumeration with the dynamic program and removing redundant work from the inner loop. At a few seconds per case on a laptop, re-planning once per horizon is within reach of the hardware a traffic-management center would have.

Limitations

The test network is a two-signal corridor with two origin–destination pairs. Demand was randomized across the 50 cases but the topology never changed, so nothing here demonstrates that the approach scales to a grid. The model assumes every vehicle is electric, connected, and willing to take the route it is given; mixed traffic, where some fraction of vehicles route themselves, is the case that matters for deployment and the one I did not handle. The randomized demand is also not calibrated against real counts, so the effect sizes support the claim that the objective matters rather than predicting what a city would save.

The price update is the part I would rebuild first. The flow and capacity terms are a proper subgradient step on the relaxed problem, but the energy term is a heuristic. It behaves well across these runs and it produces the result, but it is not derived from the Lagrangian, so convergence here is empirical rather than guaranteed. Putting energy into the objective itself and deriving the update from that would fix it.
